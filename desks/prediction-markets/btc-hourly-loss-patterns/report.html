Was there a pattern that could have avoided the losses?

One simple filter made money in this historical replay, but none is established as a reliable loss-prevention rule. Smaller positions and stopping after losses protected capital without improving the underlying predictions.

Kalshi hourly BTC contracts · September 13–October 17, 2026 UTC · $500 independent paper accounts · original decision minute 15 and hypothetical entry minute 16, leaving 44minutes before close.

The promising pattern: price already far from the strike

Take the existing combined stochastic/SMA signal only when the absolute gap between current BTC price and the contract strike is at least one estimated volatility unit for the remaining 45minutes. This kept 65 of 774 signals (8.4%), won 57 (87.69%), and ended at $650.91 after fees with 25% allocation per trade. The worst settled-cash peak-to-trough drawdown was48.28%.

This measures distance from the benchmark, not a new crossover. At minute 15, the calculation uses the last completed 5m close and the standard deviation of the last 288 complete simple 5m returns: abs(spot − strike) / (spot × rolling return std × sqrt(9)) ≥ 1. Missing/zero volatility fails closed. The volatility scale is a heuristic, not a proven normal distribution or calibrated winning probability. Coinbase spot is a proxy; Kalshi settles against the final 60-second BRTI average.

Illustration only: with an $80,000 strike and a $100 estimated volatility scale, prices at $80,100 or $79,900 meet the distance threshold. The original signal still chooses YES or NO. We did not add an agreement-with-price condition:63 of 65 retained signals happened to agree with the current side of the strike.

A separate combined-strategy 25% account that permanently halted new entries after a 10% decline from its settled-cash peak stopped after the second trade and retained$412.53. It had first risen to $549.64. A single loss then crossed the threshold, producing a 24.95% peak drawdown. A 10% halt is therefore not a 10% guaranteed loss cap when a trade can risk 25%. Why high accuracy did not protect us

The original combined strategy won 63.70% of eligible signals, but its average one-contract price plus fees was 67.27¢ per $1 payout. That produced−3.58¢ per contract. For the distance≥1 subset, observed wins were 87.69% against 85.45¢ mean cost including fees, giving+2.24¢. Buying cheaper contracts alone did not help: their win rates also fell. A reliable rule needs a calibrated probability advantage over executable price plus fees, not accuracy by itself.

Evidence and limits

The original three 25% account ledgers were reproduced field by field. Every new replay's quantity, fee, budget and settled cash was checked with exact rational arithmetic. The full mixed prediction array was reconstructed from its two components, the decision price matched the locked baseline, and all 774 source shadow observations matched. A separate previously verified engine reproduced the distance-filter result and its cost stresses.

This study does not trade, deploy, change live risk settings, or demonstrate live execution. It covers Kalshi because historical executable Polymarket quotes were not available in the accepted study. Further historical rule-search risks fitting noise; the candidate should be frozen and assessed on genuinely new market observations before adoption.
